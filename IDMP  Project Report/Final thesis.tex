{\large{\textbf{ABSTRACT}}}
\end{center}

\vspace*{1cm}

The synthesis and optimization of functional semiconductor materials increasingly demand the integration of reproducible wet-laboratory protocols with automated computational data management and analytics. This Inter-Disciplinary Mini Project (IDMP) presents a dual-track investigation combining the green, phytochemical-assisted synthesis of semiconducting copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$) thin films with the development of \textbf{GreenSynth Analytics}, a specialized research data management and materials informatics platform.

In the experimental track (Part A), nanocrystalline $\text{CuO}$ thin films are prepared through a phytochemical route utilizing mulberry (\textit{Morus alba}) leaf extract in ethanol, with copper acetate monohydrate specified as the copper precursor. Thin films are deposited onto preheated glass substrates using the chemical spray pyrolysis technique. The resulting films are evaluated through multi-technique characterization, including X-ray diffraction (XRD) for crystal structure, ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy for optical absorption and band gap determination, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy for chemical bonding, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for surface morphology, and current-voltage ($I\text{--}V$) measurements for electrical transport properties.

In the software track (Part B), GreenSynth Analytics provides a structured, full-stack digital environment designed to address data fragmentation, manual transcription errors, and observer subjectivity in materials research workflows. Built with FastAPI and scientific computing libraries, the platform features tamper-evident file ingestion with cryptographic SHA-256 verification, deterministic scientific calculation engines (automated Scherrer crystallite sizing, maximum first-derivative Tauc optical band gap extraction, and Ohmic transport regression), machine-learning regression benchmarking, and automated ReportLab PDF research dossier generation. A rigorous distinction is maintained between measured laboratory observations and physics-grounded synthetic benchmark datasets used for software testing. Together, the two tracks establish a traceable, closed-loop research workflow connecting laboratory materials synthesis with automated scientific computing.\\
\\
\textbf{Keywords}: Copper Oxide ($\text{CuO}$), Spray Pyrolysis, Phytochemical Synthesis, Mulberry Extract, Copper Acetate Monohydrate, Materials Informatics, GreenSynth Analytics, Thin Film Characterization, Data Provenance.

\section{Introduction to Project}

Scientific research increasingly involves the integration of experimental investigation with computational tools for data management, analysis, and interpretation. In materials science, the preparation of a material is only one part of the research process. Understanding its structural, optical, morphological, and electrical properties is equally important for evaluating its potential applications. The systematic recording of synthesis conditions and characterization results is therefore essential for meaningful comparisons, reproducibility, and further investigation.

The present project integrates two complementary components: the phytochemical-assisted synthesis of semiconducting copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$) thin films using the spray pyrolysis technique, and the development of \textbf{GreenSynth Analytics}, a research data management and materials analysis platform. The experimental component focuses on the preparation and characterization of $\text{CuO}$ thin films using mulberry extract and ethanol, while the software component provides a structured environment for organizing experimental information, managing characterization data, performing scientific calculations, and exploring relationships between synthesis parameters and material properties.

The project follows an interdisciplinary approach in which laboratory-based materials research is supported by software engineering, scientific computing, data visualization, statistical analysis, and machine-learning techniques. The experimental investigation provides the scientific context, while GreenSynth Analytics is intended to improve the organization, traceability, and interpretation of research data.

\subsection{Part A: Phytochemical Synthesis and Spray Pyrolysis of CuO Thin Films}

Copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$) is a semiconducting metal oxide that has attracted research interest because of its optical, electrical, and structural properties. Its potential applications include sensors, photovoltaic devices, catalytic systems, and other electronic and optoelectronic technologies. The properties of $\text{CuO}$ depend on factors such as its preparation method, composition, microstructure, substrate conditions, and subsequent processing.

Conventional material synthesis can involve different chemical precursors, solvents, and processing conditions. Phytochemical-assisted synthesis investigates the use of plant-derived extracts as part of the preparation process. Such extracts contain naturally occurring chemical constituents that may influence precursor chemistry, nucleation, growth, or surface characteristics. However, their precise role depends on the extract composition and experimental conditions and must be investigated using appropriate evidence.

In this project, mulberry extract and ethanol are used in the preparation process, with copper acetate monohydrate specified as the copper precursor. The spray pyrolysis technique is employed for the deposition of $\text{CuO}$ thin films. In this technique, a precursor solution is atomized into small droplets and transported towards a heated substrate. The droplets undergo solvent evaporation and thermal transformations, contributing to the formation of a deposited film. The resulting film characteristics depend on the solution composition and process parameters, including substrate temperature, spray rate, spray duration, nozzle-to-substrate distance, and other relevant conditions.

The experimental investigation focuses on the relationship between synthesis conditions and the properties of the resulting films. Characterization methods such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and electrical current-voltage ($I\text{--}V$) measurements can provide information about structural, optical, chemical-bonding, morphological, and electrical characteristics, respectively.

The scientific component therefore aims to establish a systematic experimental procedure, record the synthesis conditions, characterize the prepared films using the methods actually performed, and interpret the resulting observations. The findings are intended to contribute to a better understanding of the relationship between preparation conditions and the properties of $\text{CuO}$ thin films.

\subsection{Part B: GreenSynth Materials Informatics Platform}

Experimental materials research generates different forms of information, including synthesis parameters, sample identifiers, laboratory observations, characterization files, processed datasets, and calculated material properties. When this information is maintained in separate spreadsheets, files, or scripts, it can become difficult to trace results back to their original experiments, compare samples consistently, and reproduce analytical procedures.

To address these challenges, GreenSynth Analytics has been developed as a research data management and materials analysis platform. Its purpose is to provide a structured digital environment for managing research projects, experiments, synthesis parameters, samples, characterization records, raw files, and derived scientific properties.

The platform connects experimental records with subsequent data-processing and analytical workflows. Depending on the implemented functionality, these workflows can include data validation, scientific calculations, graphical visualization, statistical analysis, machine-learning model evaluation, and design-of-experiments support. Organizing these activities within a common system can help researchers investigate patterns in experimental data and compare different synthesis conditions more systematically.

GreenSynth Analytics also emphasizes data traceability by maintaining relationships between experiments, samples, characterization records, and calculated outputs. This structure supports the identification of the data and processing steps associated with a reported result. Visualization and analytical reports can further help users interpret complex datasets and communicate findings more effectively.

The platform is designed around the needs of materials research, with the $\text{CuO}$ synthesis investigation serving as a principal project context. Its architecture can potentially be extended to additional materials and synthesis techniques by introducing appropriate experimental parameters, characterization workflows, and property calculations.

An important distinction is maintained between measured laboratory data and synthetic data generated for software testing or analytical pipeline evaluation. Synthetic data can be useful for checking data integrity, testing software functionality, and benchmarking computational workflows, but it cannot establish the experimentally measured properties of $\text{CuO}$ films. Scientific conclusions must therefore be based on verified experimental measurements and appropriate analysis.

\section{Problem Statement}

Materials research involves multiple connected activities, beginning with the preparation of a material and continuing through characterization, data processing, property calculation, comparison, and interpretation. The reliability of the resulting conclusions depends on the quality of the experimental procedure, the accuracy of the measurements, and the traceability of the data used in the analysis.

In phytochemical-assisted synthesis of $\text{CuO}$ thin films, several experimental conditions may influence the resulting material properties. Understanding these relationships requires the systematic recording of preparation parameters and the analysis of characterization results. Differences in sample preparation, measurement conditions, data-processing procedures, or record-keeping practices can complicate comparisons between experiments. Furthermore, the contribution of plant-derived constituents to the synthesis process cannot be established solely from the use of a plant extract; it requires appropriate experimental and characterization evidence.

A related challenge is the management of the data generated during the research process. Synthesis records, sample details, raw characterization files, calculated properties, and analytical results may exist in different formats and locations. Without a structured system, connecting a reported property to its original sample, experimental conditions, raw data, and calculation procedure can become difficult. Repeated manual processing may also introduce inconsistencies and reduce the efficiency of data comparison.

The project therefore addresses two interconnected problems:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scientific Problem:} To investigate the preparation and characterization of phytochemical-assisted $\text{CuO}$ thin films and examine how documented synthesis conditions relate to their structural, optical, morphological, and electrical properties.
    \item \textbf{Software Problem:} To develop a structured research platform that organizes experimental data, maintains relationships between experiments and samples, supports scientific data processing and visualization, and provides analytical functionality for systematic comparison and investigation.
\end{enumerate}

GreenSynth Analytics is intended to address the software-related challenges while supporting the broader scientific investigation. The success of the software must be assessed through implementation and testing evidence, whereas conclusions about the synthesized material must be supported independently by verified laboratory data.

\section{Scope and Objectives}

The project combines experimental materials science with the development of a scientific software platform. Its scope includes the preparation and investigation of $\text{CuO}$ thin films, the management of associated research data, and the implementation and evaluation of relevant analytical workflows.

\subsection{Scope}

\noindent\textbf{A. Scientific and Experimental Scope}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Preparation of mulberry extract using the documented laboratory extraction procedure, including Soxhlet extraction where performed.
    \item Preparation of the precursor solution using copper acetate monohydrate and ethanol according to the recorded experimental procedure.
    \item Deposition of $\text{CuO}$ thin films using spray pyrolysis.
    \item Recording of relevant synthesis parameters and experimental observations.
    \item Characterization of the prepared films using the techniques actually performed, including XRD, UV-Vis, FTIR, SEM, and electrical $I\text{--}V$ measurements where data are available.
    \item Processing and interpretation of characterization data using appropriate scientific methods.
    \item Investigation of relationships between synthesis conditions and material properties, subject to the availability and quality of experimental measurements.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{B. Software Development Scope}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Structured management of research projects, experiments, synthesis parameters, and samples.
    \item Storage and organization of characterization records and raw research files.
    \item Association of raw data with relevant experiments and samples.
    \item Support for scientific calculations and derived-property records.
    \item Visualization and comparison of research data.
    \item Statistical analysis and machine-learning workflows, where implemented.
    \item Evaluation of predictive models using appropriate validation methods.
    \item Design-of-experiments and candidate-condition analysis, where implemented.
    \item Data integrity, access control, validation, and testing of relevant software functions.
    \item Preparation of research outputs and analytical reports for interpretation and communication.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{C. Scope Limitations}\\
The project is limited to the materials, synthesis methods, characterization techniques, and software functions included in the actual implementation and experimental work. Additional materials, instruments, synthesis methods, or analytical features are outside the demonstrated scope unless separately implemented and evaluated.

Machine-learning outputs and proposed experimental conditions are treated as analytical support rather than definitive scientific conclusions. Their reliability depends on the dataset, experimental coverage, model-validation procedure, and applicability to the conditions being investigated. Synthetic datasets used for software testing are kept distinct from experimental measurements.

\subsection{Objectives}

\noindent\textbf{A. Scientific Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To investigate the phytochemical-assisted synthesis of semiconducting $\text{CuO}$ thin films using mulberry extract and the spray pyrolysis technique.
    \item To prepare the precursor solution using the specified copper precursor and solvent according to the documented experimental procedure.
    \item To identify and record the synthesis parameters relevant to the preparation of $\text{CuO}$ thin films.

\chapter{LITERATURE REVIEW}

\section{Existing Systems}

Existing research relevant to the present project can be divided into two major areas: phytochemical-assisted synthesis and characterization of copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$), and software platforms for scientific data management and materials informatics. Reviewing both areas establishes the scientific background for the experimental investigation and the technological context for GreenSynth Analytics.

\subsection{Existing Approaches to Phytochemical-Assisted CuO Synthesis}

Plant-mediated synthesis of metal and metal oxide nanomaterials has received considerable research attention because plant extracts contain diverse chemical constituents that may participate in material formation or influence particle growth and surface properties. Phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and other phytochemicals have been investigated for their possible roles in reducing, complexing, capping, or stabilizing species during synthesis. However, the specific role of these constituents depends on the plant source, extraction procedure, precursor chemistry, and reaction conditions.

Siddiqi and Husen (2020) reviewed plant-metabolite-based approaches for the fabrication of copper and copper oxide nanoparticles. Their review describes the use of different plant sources and discusses the relevance of phytochemicals to nanoparticle formation and stabilization. The diversity of plant extracts and synthesis conditions indicates that experimental results obtained using one botanical source cannot automatically be generalized to another.

A review by \textit{New Frontiers in the Plant Extract Mediated Biosynthesis of Copper Oxide (CuO) Nanoparticles and Their Potential Applications} (2022) examined plant-extract-assisted approaches to $\text{CuO}$ nanoparticle synthesis, factors influencing the process, and possible applications. The review also identified challenges associated with understanding synthesis mechanisms and achieving consistent material properties. These observations support the need to record experimental conditions carefully and interpret characterization results using evidence rather than assumptions.

The present project investigates a specific preparation route involving mulberry (\textit{Morus alba}) extract, ethanol, copper acetate monohydrate, and spray pyrolysis. Its scientific scope concerns $\text{CuO}$ thin films rather than assuming that findings for solution-synthesized nanoparticles necessarily apply to deposited films. The resulting material properties must therefore be evaluated using the actual preparation and characterization procedures employed in this project.

\subsection{Existing Synthesis and Characterization Techniques}

Different physical and chemical methods are used to prepare copper oxide materials, including solution-based synthesis, precipitation, hydrothermal processing, and deposition techniques. The choice of method influences the processing conditions, form of the resulting material, and suitability for the intended application.

Spray pyrolysis is a deposition technique in which an atomized precursor solution is transported towards a heated substrate. Solvent evaporation and thermal transformations contribute to film formation. The resulting properties can depend on precursor composition, substrate temperature, droplet transport, spray rate, deposition duration, and other operating conditions.

Material characterization is required to establish the properties of the prepared film. X-ray diffraction (XRD) is used to investigate crystal structure and phase information. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy provides optical measurements that may be used for band-gap estimation when appropriate assumptions and data transformations are applied. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) can provide evidence about vibrational modes and functional groups. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is used to examine surface morphology, while electrical current-voltage ($I\text{--}V$) measurements can support the evaluation of electrical behavior.

These techniques provide different and complementary forms of evidence. No single technique establishes every property of a material. For example, surface morphology observed through SEM does not by itself establish chemical composition, and a proposed phytochemical mechanism cannot be confirmed solely from the use of a plant extract.

Consequently, the scientific component of the present project requires a traceable connection between preparation conditions, sample identity, raw characterization data, processing procedures, and derived properties.

\subsection{Existing Materials Data and Informatics Platforms}

Materials research increasingly uses digital databases, scientific software, and computational methods to manage information and investigate relationships between material composition, processing conditions, structure, and properties.

Hill et al. (2016) discussed the growing importance of large-scale data and informatics in materials science. Their work highlighted challenges such as fragmented data resources, inconsistent data standards, and difficulties in making heterogeneous scientific information accessible and reusable. The study also described materials-data resources and the potential role of analytical platforms in accelerating materials research.

The Materials Project is an established open platform that provides access to computational materials data and related tools. It supports the exploration of material properties and provides programmatic access to data for further analysis. Its principal emphasis is on materials data and computationally derived properties rather than serving as a dedicated laboratory workflow for the specific $\text{CuO}$ synthesis process investigated here.

Citrine Informatics and its Citrination platform represent another approach to materials data infrastructure and informatics. O'Mara, Meredig, and Michel (2016) examined the design of a materials data infrastructure, including the import, storage, and access of structured and unstructured information. Such platforms illustrate the importance of connecting scientific records with interfaces and analytical tools.

The Materials Data Facility, described by Blaiszik et al. (2016), supports materials research data publication and discovery. Its approach includes metadata, programmatic interfaces, data discovery, and the sharing of research datasets. These functions demonstrate how structured metadata and consistent data access can improve the reuse and discoverability of scientific information.

Rumble (2017) discussed broader challenges in accessing materials data, including fragmentation across resources, interoperability, and data quality. The review emphasized the importance of findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability, commonly referred to as the FAIR principles.

These systems provide important precedents for scientific data management and materials informatics. However, their objectives and intended users differ. A general materials database, a computational property repository, a data-publishing infrastructure, and a project-specific experimental research platform do not necessarily provide identical functionality.

\subsection{Existing Data Analysis and Visualization Approaches}

Scientific data analysis commonly combines numerical processing, statistical methods, graphical visualization, and predictive modeling. Python-based scientific libraries provide tools for data transformation, numerical calculations, statistical analysis, and machine learning. Visualization tools can represent distributions, correlations, trends, and comparisons between experimental conditions.

Materials-informatics research demonstrates that structured data can be used to investigate process-property relationships and develop predictive models. However, the usefulness of such models depends on data quality, sample coverage, appropriate validation, and the distinction between measured and calculated quantities.

Data visualization platforms, including Power BI, can provide interactive reports, filtering, summaries, and comparisons. These tools are useful for communicating research information, but they do not replace specialized scientific processing. For example, a plotted band-gap value must still be derived using an appropriate scientific method, and an electrical property must still be calculated from valid measurements and geometry.

GreenSynth Analytics follows this broader approach by combining structured research records with scientific processing and visualization. Its specific contribution must be evaluated against the functions actually implemented rather than assumed from the capabilities of other platforms.

\section{Limitations of Existing Systems}

Although existing synthesis methods, scientific databases, and materials-informatics platforms provide valuable capabilities, several challenges remain relevant to the present project.

\subsection{Limitations in Phytochemical-Assisted Synthesis}

Plant-mediated synthesis involves natural extracts whose chemical composition can vary with botanical source, growing conditions, plant part, extraction method, and storage. These variations may influence synthesis outcomes and complicate comparisons between studies.

Other challenges include the need to control processing conditions, achieve reproducible material characteristics, and distinguish proposed chemical mechanisms from experimentally established ones. The use of a plant extract alone does not establish that a synthesis route is environmentally preferable in every respect; solvent use, energy consumption, waste generation, and processing requirements also need consideration.

In addition, studies involving nanoparticles cannot automatically be treated as direct evidence for thin-film deposition. Differences in material form and preparation technique affect the interpretation of the findings. The present project must therefore evaluate the specific mulberry-extract and spray-pyrolysis procedure used for its $\text{CuO}$ thin films.

\subsection{Limitations in Experimental Data Management}

Research information can be distributed across spreadsheets, instrument-generated files, laboratory notebooks, and separate processing scripts. When these resources are not connected through consistent identifiers and metadata, it can be difficult to trace a calculated property to the original sample and experimental conditions.

Differences in file formats, units, parameter names, and data-processing procedures can also make direct comparisons difficult. Inadequate recording of processing steps may reduce reproducibility, while incomplete metadata may prevent researchers from determining how a result was produced.

Materials-data infrastructure research has identified fragmentation, interoperability, and data-quality challenges as important issues in scientific data access and reuse (Hill et al., 2016; Rumble, 2017).

\subsection{Limitations in Existing Materials Informatics Platforms}

Existing materials-data systems serve different purposes, and no single limitation applies equally to all of them. Public materials repositories may focus on curated or computationally generated properties, while data-publishing platforms emphasize discovery, sharing, and metadata. Specialized commercial systems may provide broader experimental-data and analytics functionality.

For the present project, the relevant design challenge is to support the complete workflow required by the specific research context: recording synthesis conditions, associating samples with characterization files, preserving processing information, storing derived properties, and comparing analytical outputs.

A general database or visualization tool may not independently provide all the domain-specific calculations and sample-to-experiment relationships required for this workflow. Such functionality may require additional configuration, integration, or custom development. This is a design consideration for GreenSynth Analytics, not a claim that existing platforms universally lack these capabilities.

\subsection{Limitations in Data-Driven Analysis}

Statistical analysis and machine-learning models can help identify relationships in experimental data, but their conclusions depend on the quantity, quality, and representativeness of the available records. Small datasets, inconsistent experimental procedures, missing values, and duplicated or related samples can lead to misleading evaluation results.

A model may also perform well on data similar to its training records while failing to generalize to new experimental conditions. Evaluation should therefore use appropriate validation procedures, relevant baseline models, and metrics suited to the prediction task.

Furthermore, correlations between synthesis parameters and material properties do not independently establish causation. Predictions generated from synthetic data can help test a software pipeline but cannot validate the physical behavior of an actual $\text{CuO}$ synthesis process.

\subsection{Limitations in Research Visualization and Reporting}

Visualization tools can make data exploration easier, but the quality of a report depends on the correctness of the underlying data and the suitability of the chosen visual representation. A dashboard may summarize results effectively while concealing important information about uncertainty, missing observations, measurement conditions, or sample selection.

General-purpose reporting tools also require a reliable connection to the underlying data source, suitable data models, correct calculations, and appropriate access controls. Power BI can provide an interactive reporting layer when integrated, but it does not independently replace the scientific calculations and validation procedures performed by the research system.

These limitations motivate a design in which data integrity, scientific processing, traceability, and visualization are treated as connected but distinct responsibilities. \section{Proposed System}

The proposed system, \textbf{GreenSynth Analytics}, is a research data management and materials analysis platform developed in the context of phytochemical-assisted synthesis and characterization of $\text{CuO}$ thin films. It aims to connect experimental records, characterization data, scientific calculations, and supported analytical workflows within a structured digital environment.

The system is designed to maintain relationships between projects, experiments, synthesis parameters, samples, characterization records, raw files, analysis operations, and derived material properties. This structure allows research information to be organized consistently and supports the traceability of analytical outputs to their corresponding experimental records.

The major functional areas of the proposed system are described below:

\subsection{Research Project and Experiment Management}
The platform organizes research projects and experimental records. Each experiment can be associated with its relevant synthesis conditions and samples, allowing results to be compared in the context of the conditions under which the material was prepared.

\subsection{Sample and Characterization Management}
The system links samples with their characterization records and associated raw files. Different characterization techniques may generate different types of data, so the platform must preserve relevant metadata and avoid treating distinct measurement types as interchangeable.

\subsection{Scientific Data Processing}
The platform supports scientific calculations and data-processing workflows where implemented. Processing procedures should identify the source data, applicable method, relevant parameters, units, and output so that derived values can be interpreted and checked.

\subsection{Visualization and Comparative Analysis}
GreenSynth Analytics provides a foundation for visualizing and comparing research data. Graphical analysis can help researchers explore distributions, relationships, and differences between experimental conditions. If Power BI is integrated, it can provide additional interactive reporting and dashboard functionality while the scientific calculations remain grounded in the underlying data-processing workflow.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis and Machine Learning}
Where implemented, statistical and machine-learning functionality can be used to investigate relationships between synthesis parameters and material properties. Model evaluation should include appropriate validation procedures, comparison with baseline approaches, and consideration of uncertainty and applicability to new conditions.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Research Support}
Design-of-experiments methods can support the systematic comparison of candidate experimental conditions. Proposed conditions or model-generated predictions are intended to guide further investigation rather than replace laboratory testing. Their usefulness must be assessed through suitable experimental validation.

\subsection{Data Integrity and Reproducibility}
The proposed system emphasizes the preservation of relationships between original records, raw files, processing operations, and derived outputs. It also requires appropriate access control and data validation to protect research records and maintain consistency.

Synthetic datasets may be used to test software functionality and analytical workflows. Such records must be identified clearly and must not be presented as experimental measurements.

Overall, GreenSynth Analytics is intended to connect materials research with structured data management and computational analysis. Its performance and contribution must be established through implementation evidence, software testing, and the scientific validity of results obtained from real laboratory measurements.

\section{SDLC Model}

The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) provides a structured approach to planning, designing, implementing, testing, deploying, and maintaining software systems. Selecting an appropriate development model is important because GreenSynth Analytics combines user-facing workflows, database operations, scientific processing, data visualization, and analytical functionality.

An \textbf{Iterative and Incremental Development Model} is suitable for a project of this nature because the platform can be developed in functional stages, with each stage reviewed and tested before further functionality is added. This approach allows requirements, data models, and analytical workflows to be refined as the experimental and software components become better understood.

\subsection{Requirements Analysis}
The first stage identifies the scientific workflow, user needs, functional requirements, non-functional requirements, and constraints. Requirements include the management of projects, experiments, samples, characterization data, analytical operations, and user access.

\subsection{System and Database Design}
The requirements are translated into the system architecture, database schema, API structure, user workflows, and access-control rules. The design establishes how experiments, samples, files, and calculated properties are connected.

\subsection{Incremental Implementation}
The platform is implemented in functional modules. Core data management and authentication can be established first, followed by experiment and sample workflows, characterization-file handling, scientific calculations, visualization, and supported analytical functions. Each increment should be checked against its requirements before additional functionality is introduced.

\subsection{Testing and Verification}
Each module is tested independently and in combination with other components. Testing includes input validation, database integrity, API behavior, access control, scientific calculations, file handling, and end-to-end research workflows.

\subsection{Integration and Deployment}
The modules are integrated into the complete platform and configured for the intended environment. Database connectivity, environment variables, storage access, user permissions, and operational behavior must be verified before the system is considered ready for use.

\subsection{Evaluation and Maintenance}
The system is reviewed against its requirements, and defects or usability issues are addressed. Future changes may include additional characterization workflows, analytical functions, report integrations, and support for additional materials.

\subsection{Suitability of the Iterative and Incremental Model}
The iterative and incremental model is appropriate when the project requires gradual integration of multiple technical components and repeated testing of research workflows. It supports early verification of core functionality and allows later analytical modules to be added without requiring every feature to be completed before testing begins.

However, iterative development does not remove the need for documented requirements, version control, testing, data integrity, and security review. These activities remain necessary throughout development. For GreenSynth Analytics, the development model provides a framework for organizing the software into manageable increments and validating each increment against the research requirements. The resulting platform must still be evaluated using documented tests and actual implementation evidence. 


\chapter{REQUIREMENT SPECIFICATION}

\section{Functional Requirements}

The functional requirements define the core operational capabilities provided by the GreenSynth Analytics platform to support experimental research workflows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{User Authentication and Access Control:} Provide secure user registration, token-based session management, and role-based permissions (Administrator, Researcher, Viewer) to protect proprietary research data.
    \item \textbf{Research Project Management:} Create, organize, and archive multi-investigator research projects, maintaining isolated project namespaces and team assignments.
    \item \textbf{Experiment and Synthesis Logging:} Record comprehensive synthesis recipes, including chemical precursors (copper acetate monohydrate, ethanol, mulberry extract), operational parameters (substrate temperature, spray rate, carrier gas pressure), and real-time laboratory observations.
    \item \textbf{Sample Identity and Lineage Tracking:} Assign persistent, unique identifiers to physical thin-film specimens and trace their hierarchical parentage back to specific synthesis experiments.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Modal Characterization Ingestion:} Support multipart file uploads for raw instrumental data across five distinct techniques: X-ray diffraction (XRD), ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and current-voltage ($I\text{--}V$) transport sweeps.
    \item \textbf{Automated Scientific Derivation Engines:} Execute deterministic mathematical processing pipelines on uploaded raw spectra to calculate physical material properties:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Automatic polynomial background subtraction and Scherrer crystallite size ($D$) calculation from XRD peaks.
        \item Automated photon energy ($h\nu$) conversion, Tauc coordinate transformation ($(\alpha h\nu)^2$), and band gap ($E_g$) extraction via maximum first-derivative tangent fitting.
        \item Linear least-squares regression over $I\text{--}V$ sweeps to calculate electrical resistance ($R$), sheet resistance ($R_s$), and conductivity ($\sigma$).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interactive Data Visualization:} Render interactive spectral curves, multi-sample overlays, parameter distribution histograms, and property correlation scatter plots directly within the user interface.
    \item \textbf{Predictive Machine-Learning Benchmarking:} Train, evaluate, and benchmark cross-validated regression models predicting functional properties from synthesis variables, guarded by automated target data leakage prevention mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Standardized Scientific Dossier Generation:} Compile automated, publication-ready research reports in multi-page PDF format utilizing ReportLab, capturing data provenance, mathematical derivations, and tabular summaries.
    \item \textbf{Cryptographic Data Integrity Verification:} Generate SHA-256 cryptographic digests upon file upload to ensure tamper detection and guarantee research reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Non Functional Requirements}

The platform adheres to stringent software quality standards to ensure usability, security, and scientific reliability:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Security and Access Protection:} All API communications must be encrypted over HTTPS. User authentication tokens must utilize industry-standard cryptographic signatures (JWT), and raw data uploads must be fingerprinted using SHA-256 digests to detect unauthorized post-measurement alterations.
    \item \textbf{System Reliability and Fault Tolerance:} The backend services must gracefully handle malformed data files, irregular instrumental headers, and network interruptions without crashing, returning informative HTTP error responses with actionable diagnostics.
    \item \textbf{Performance and Response Latency:} Standard database queries and API endpoints must respond within $200\text{ ms}$ under typical laboratory loads. Deterministic scientific derivation routines (Scherrer, Tauc, Ohmic regression) must execute in under $50\text{ ms}$ per spectral dataset.
    \item \textbf{Usability and Interface Clarity:} The user interface must provide responsive, intuitive navigation across desktop and tablet devices, displaying progress indicators during file uploads and presenting scientific charts with accessible units and legends.
    \item \textbf{Maintainability and Modularity:} The software architecture must follow a modular, service-oriented design with clear separation between API routing, business logic, data models, and derivation engines, supported by comprehensive automated test suites.
    \item \textbf{Scalability:} The relational schema must support thousands of experiments, samples, and high-frequency spectral arrays without degradation in query performance, leveraging database indexing and connection pooling.
    \item \textbf{Data Integrity and Traceability:} Relational integrity must be enforced via foreign keys, unique constraints, and database cascading rules. Every derived property must maintain an unbroken audit link to its parent sample, raw file, and mathematical algorithm version.
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility:} Scientific derivations must be purely deterministic: processing the same raw data file with identical parameters must yield identical calculated numerical outputs regardless of execution environment.

The relational data model of GreenSynth Analytics is normalized to Third Normal Form (3NF) to eliminate update anomalies, ensure referential integrity, and maintain strict lineage between physical laboratory samples and digital analytical runs.

The database schema is organized across five cohesive architectural domains:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Access and Governance Domain:} The \texttt{USERS} entity tracks system operators, passwords (bcrypt hashes), and assigned roles. Each user belongs to one or more \texttt{RESEARCH\_GROUPS} via the associative table \texttt{GROUP\_MEMBERSHIPS}. All administrative and state-changing actions generate timestamped entries in \texttt{AUDIT\_LOGS}.
    \item \textbf{Project, Experiment, and Sample Hierarchy:} A \texttt{PROJECTS} entity establishes the overarching research scope (e.g., green-synthesized thin-film semiconductors). A project owns dynamic \texttt{PARAMETER\_DEFINITIONS} (which configure admissible minimum and maximum bounds for physical factors) and multiple \texttt{EXPERIMENTS}. Each experiment records specific synthesis run conditions across up to 15 factors in \texttt{EXPERIMENT\_PARAMETERS} and produces one or more physical \texttt{SAMPLES}.
    \item \textbf{Characterization and Analysis Engine Domain:} Each sample is measured across multiple characterization techniques stored in \texttt{CHARACTERIZATIONS}. The raw data streams from analytical instruments are preserved in \texttt{RAW\_FILES}, which maintains the original filename, storage URI, and a SHA-256 cryptographic digest. Computational analysis runs (\texttt{ANALYSIS\_RUNS}) execute deterministic derivation routines, generating extracted \texttt{XRD\_PEAKS} and physical parameters stored in \texttt{CALCULATED\_PROPERTIES} (e.g., crystallite size $D$, optical band gap $E_g$, electrical resistivity $\rho$, and conductivity $\sigma$).
    \item \textbf{Machine Learning and Model Registry Domain:} Curated synthesis and characterization vectors are compiled into \texttt{ML\_DATASETS} and individual rows in \texttt{ML\_DATASET\_RECORDS}. Supervised regressors are tracked in \texttt{ML\_MODELS}, their 5-fold cross-validation scores documented in \texttt{ML\_TRAINING\_RUNS}, and predictive inferences logged in \texttt{ML\_PREDICTIONS}.
    \item \textbf{Design of Experiments (DOE) Domain:} Systematic experimental optimization matrices are configured in \texttt{DOES}. When an orthogonal array or response surface design is computed, proposed synthesis conditions are written to \texttt{PROPOSED\_}\allowbreak\texttt{EXPERIMENTS}, which can subsequently be instantiated as active lab experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{DFD Level 2: Subsystem 3.0 (Characterization \& Scientific Engine)}

Figure~\ref{fig:dfd_level2_char} details the data flow within Process 3.0 as raw experimental files pass from initial ingestion to derived physical constants and curated ML feature matrices.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=1.8\textheight,keepaspectratio]{"mermaid-diagram-2026-10-09-083238.png"}
\caption{DFD Level 2: Subsystem 3.0 (Characterization \& Scientific Derivation Engine)}
\label{fig:dfd_level2_char}
\end{center}
\end{figure}

The sub-processes in Subsystem 3.0 include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Process 3.1 (File Ingestion \& SHA-256 Checksum):} Validates file format (.csv, .txt), computes a SHA-256 hash to ensure provenance, stores the binary stream into the secure file vault, and registers metadata in Data Store \textbf{D1 (raw\_files)}.
    \item \textbf{Process 3.2 (XRD Peak Detection \& Scherrer Engine):} Parses $2\theta$-intensity arrays, removes continuous background radiation, identifies diffraction peaks, fits Gaussian/Lorentzian profiles to compute Full Width at Half Maximum ($\beta$), and calculates crystallite size ($D$) via the Debye-Scherrer equation ($D = \frac{K\lambda}{\beta \cos\theta}$). Peak locations are written to \textbf{D2 (xrd\_peaks)} and crystallite size to \textbf{D3 (calculated\_properties)}.
    \item \textbf{Process 3.3 (UV-Vis Tauc Band Gap Extrapolation Engine):} Transforms wavelength ($\lambda$) and absorbance ($A$) into photon energy ($h\nu$) and absorption coefficient ($\alpha$). Constructs $(\alpha h\nu)^2$ vs.\ $h\nu$ arrays, locates the maximum linear slope region, and extrapolates the tangent line to the abscissa intercept ($\alpha h\nu = 0$) to compute direct optical band gap ($E_g$), persisted in \textbf{D3}.
    \item \textbf{Process 3.4 (Electrical I-V Ohmic Conductivity Engine):} Accepts current-voltage sweeps, executes linear least-squares regression to extract sheet resistance ($R$), and applies sample geometry (film thickness $t$ and contact dimensions) to calculate electrical resistivity ($\rho$) and electrical conductivity ($\sigma = 1/\rho$), stored in \textbf{D3}.
    \item \textbf{Process 3.5 (ML Feature Assembler \& Leakage Guard):} Joins physical synthesis parameters from \textbf{D4 (experiment\_parameters)} with verified target properties from \textbf{D3}, enforces a strict leakage prevention rule (excluding intermediate characterizations from input features), and packages clean tensors into Data Store \textbf{D5 (ml\_datasets \& records)}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{DFD Level 2: Subsystem 4.0 (Machine Learning \& DOE Optimization)}

Figure~\ref{fig:dfd_level2_ml} illustrates the data flows governing feature normalization, cross-validation, champion model selection, applicability domain validation, and orthogonal experimental design generation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.94\textheight,keepaspectratio]{"mermaid-diagram-2026-10-09-083343.png"}

\label{fig:dfd_level2_ml}
\end{center}

The sub-processes in Subsystem 4.0 execute in the following sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Process 4.1 (Feature Preprocessing \& Leakage Guard):} Pulls target properties ($\sigma$) from \textbf{D3} and synthesis variables from \textbf{D4}. Performs MinMax feature scaling and standardization, guaranteeing that no target-correlated characterization variables leak into the input space. Persists the normalized matrix in \textbf{D5 (ml\_datasets)}.
    \item \textbf{Process 4.2 (5-Fold Cross-Validation \& Model Training):} Partitions the training matrix into 5 stratified folds. Fits Random Forest Regressor, Gradient Boosting Regressor, and XGBoost models. Model binaries are serialized (.joblib) into local storage, and fold metrics ($R^2$, RMSE, MAE) are recorded in \textbf{D6 (ml\_models \& runs)}.

    \item \textbf{Process 4.4 (Prediction Inference \& Domain Bounds Check):} Accepts arbitrary synthesis queries from researchers, projects candidate vectors into the trained feature space, checks Mahalanobis distance / bounding-box limits to verify applicability domain validity, and calculates point predictions with 95\% confidence intervals, saving results to \textbf{D7 (ml\_predictions)}.
    \item \textbf{Process 4.5 (DOE Matrix \& Candidate Recommendation):} Ingests optimization goals (e.g., maximize conductivity $\sigma$), explores high-scoring predicted regimes, and constructs an orthogonal experimental array ($L_9 / L_{16}$) that balances parameter space exploration with exploitation. Writes candidate runs into Data Store \textbf{D8 (does \& proposed\_experiments)}.
\end{enumerate}

The system is partitioned into a decoupled architecture comprising an interactive client-side Single-Page Application (SPA), a high-throughput asynchronous backend server, an enterprise relational database management system, and an integrated scientific computation and machine-learning engine.

\section{Algorithm}

\subsection{Data Management, Validation, and Transformation Procedures}

GreenSynth Analytics enforces a deterministic data processing pipeline that ensures data integrity from raw laboratory instrument ingestion through to finalized physical property derivation and predictive modeling.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input Validation and Boundary Enforcement:} When a researcher logs synthesis parameters (e.g., precursor molarity, solvent ratio, spray pyrolysis substrate temperature), the data payload is validated against strict Pydantic schemas. If a parameter exceeds physical boundaries (e.g., substrate temperature $< 100^\circ\text{C}$ or $> 600^\circ\text{C}$), the transaction is rejected with descriptive error payloads.
    \item \textbf{Cryptographic Hashing and Deduplication:} Uploaded characterization files are processed via a streaming SHA-256 hashing algorithm. If a file with an identical hash already exists within the target project scope, the system flags the upload to avoid redundant storage and prevent dataset duplication.
    \item \textbf{Instrument Header Sniffing and Sanitization:} Raw instrument ASCII data are inspected line-by-line. The parser identifies variable-length metadata header lines (instrument model, scan rate, voltage, tube type), determines delimiters (comma, tab, whitespace), scrubs non-numeric tokens, and parses numeric arrays into normalized coordinate vectors.
    \item \textbf{Signal Conditioning and Background Subtraction:} Raw spectral data undergo digital signal conditioning. A Savitzky-Golay filter removes high-frequency detector scintillation without compromising legitimate peak morphology. Baseline drift caused by substrate scattering is modeled and subtracted using asymmetric least squares.
    \item \textbf{Physical Property Extraction:} Domain-specific algorithms extract material properties (crystallite size, optical bandgap, electrical conductivity), which are validated against expected semiconductor bounds and persisted to the relational database.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Core Scientific Derivation Algorithms}

The platform implements five primary computational algorithms:

\subsubsection{Algorithm 1: Cryptographic Ingestion and Tabular Stream Parsing}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stream Hashing:} As bytes are streamed from the client, a running SHA-256 digest is accumulated:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{digest} = \mathcal{H}_{\text{SHA-256}}(\text{raw\_byte\_stream})
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Deduplication Check:} Query database index on \texttt{raw\_files.checksum\_sha256}. If matching record exists, attach reference or raise collision warning.
    \item \textbf{Delimiter and Header Detection:} Read leading lines to detect instrument metadata tokens. Extract numeric columns into coordinate array $\mathbf{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$.
    \item \textbf{Sanitization:} Filter out non-numeric values, NaN tokens, and sensor over-range artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Algorithm 2: Automated XRD Scherrer Crystallite Size Derivation}
The routine extracts average crystallite size ($D$) along dominant diffraction planes using the Scherrer formula:
\begin{equation}
    D = \frac{K \lambda}{\beta \cos\theta}
    \label{eq:scherrer_formula}
\end{equation}
where $K = 0.94$ is the crystallite shape factor, $\lambda = 0.15406\text{ nm}$ ($\text{Cu-}K\alpha$ radiation), $\theta$ is the Bragg diffraction angle in radians, and $\beta$ is the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) in radians, corrected for instrumental broadening:
\begin{equation}
    \beta = \sqrt{\beta_{\text{observed}}^2 - \beta_{\text{instrumental}}^2}
\end{equation}
\noindent\textbf{Execution Workflow:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Apply Savitzky-Golay digital filter (window length $= 7$, polynomial order $= 2$) to smooth raw counts without peak distortion.
    \item Identify peak maxima where $\frac{dI}{d(2\theta)} = 0$ and $\frac{d^2I}{d(2\theta)^2} < 0$, filtering candidates by prominence threshold ($P \ge 0.15 \times I_{\max}$).
    \item Fit peak profiles—specifically the monoclinic $\text{CuO}$ $(\bar{1}11)$ and $(111)$ reflections at $2\theta \approx 35.5^\circ$ and $38.7^\circ$—with a Pseudo-Voigt distribution to extract centroid $2\theta_0$ and $\beta$.
    \item Evaluate Equation (\ref{eq:scherrer_formula}) and store derived crystallite size in \texttt{calculated\_properties}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Algorithm 3: Automated UV-Vis Tauc Optical Bandgap Extraction}
For direct allowed interband transitions in semiconducting $\text{CuO}$, the optical absorption coefficient $\alpha$ near the absorption edge obeys Tauc's relation:
\begin{equation}
    (\alpha h\nu)^2 = A (h\nu - E_g)
    \label{eq:tauc_equation}
\end{equation}
where $h\nu = \frac{1239.8}{\lambda\ (\text{nm})}$ represents photon energy in $\text{eV}$, $A$ is an energy-independent constant, and $E_g$ is the optical bandgap.
\noindent\textbf{Execution Workflow:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Convert experimental wavelength $\lambda$ and absorbance $A_{\text{abs}}$ into photon energy $h\nu$ and $(\alpha h\nu)^2$.
    \item Compute numerical first derivative $\frac{d}{d(h\nu)}[(\alpha h\nu)^2]$ to identify the maximum slope coordinate (inflection point $h\nu_{\text{infl}}$).
    \item Fit a linear regression line $y = m(h\nu) + c$ over the linear absorption window surrounding $h\nu_{\text{infl}}$.
    \item Determine the $x$-intercept at $(\alpha h\nu)^2 = 0$, yielding the direct bandgap:
    \begin{equation}
        E_g = -\frac{c}{m} \quad (\text{eV})
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Algorithm 4: Steady-State Electrical I--V Ohmic Regression and Conductivity Derivation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Ingest voltage-current coordinate vectors $(V_i, I_i)$ from Keithley 2450 SourceMeter scans across the linear sweep window $[-10\text{ V}, +10\text{ V}]$.
    \item Fit linear regression:
    \begin{equation}
        I = \left(\frac{1}{R}\right) V + I_{\text{offset}}
    \end{equation}
    Evaluate $R^2 \ge 0.998$ to confirm Ohmic behavior.
    \item Calculate film electrical conductivity $\sigma$ using electrode length $L$, gap $W$, and thickness $t \approx 400\text{ nm}$:
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma = \frac{L}{R \cdot W \cdot t} \quad (\text{S/cm})
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Algorithm 5: Machine Learning Predictive Modeling \& Cross-Validation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Assemble feature vectors $\mathbf{x} = [T_{\text{sub}}, M, C_{\text{ext}}, Q, \text{pH}]$ from \texttt{experiment\_parameters}.
    \item Target vectors $\mathbf{y}$ include bandgap ($E_g$), crystallite size ($D$), and conductivity ($\sigma$).
    \item Apply 5-fold cross-validation across Random Forest and XGBoost regressors, tracking $R^2$, RMSE, and MAE across training and validation folds.
    \item Store model weights and validation metrics in \texttt{ml\_models} and \texttt{ml\_training\_runs}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Physical Materials Synthesis Implementation}

To validate the digital informatics pipeline with real laboratory data, experimental synthesis was conducted investigating phytochemical-assisted $\text{CuO}$ thin films deposited via chemical spray pyrolysis.

\subsubsection{Precursor Chemistry and Stoichiometric Calculations}
Analytical reagent grade copper sulfate pentahydrate ($\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $M_r = 249.68\text{ g/mol}$) was reacted with sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$, $M_r = 40.00\text{ g/mol}$) in the presence of filtered \textit{Morus alba} (mulberry) leaf extract in ethanol:
\begin{align}
    \text{CuSO}_4(aq) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) &\xrightarrow{\text{phytochemicals}} \text{Cu(OH)}_2(s)\downarrow + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) \\
    \text{Cu(OH)}_2(s) &\xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CuO}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)\uparrow
\end{align}
In a representative laboratory trial, $7.49\text{ g}$ of $\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$ was dissolved in $75\text{ mL}$ deionized water ($0.40\text{ M}$), combined with $75\text{ mL}$ of mulberry leaf extract, and titrated with $2.40\text{ g}$ $\text{NaOH}$ in $60\text{ mL}$ water ($1.00\text{ M}$) at $70^\circ\text{C}$ for $30\text{ min}$. The monitored pH dynamic trajectory evolved from $2.45$ (pure $\text{CuSO}_4$), to $2.60$ upon extract addition, reaching $12.36$ upon complete titration.

\subsubsection{Chemical Spray Pyrolysis Deposition Envelope}
The stabilized precursor solution was atomized onto pre-cleaned borosilicate glass substrates held at $350^\circ\text{C}\text{--}400^\circ\text{C}$ using clean compressed air carrier gas ($1.8\text{ bar}$) at a liquid flow rate of $3.5\text{ mL/min}$ over a nozzle-substrate distance of $28.0\text{ cm}$. Deposition proceeded for $25\text{ min}$ with a $10\text{ s ON} / 15\text{ s OFF}$ duty cycle, preventing thermal quenching and producing adherent, nanocrystalline $\text{CuO}$ thin films.

\subsection{Workflow}

The platform realizes a unified, 12-stage end-to-end research workflow, depicted structurally below:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1: User Authentication and Access Authorization:} Researchers authenticate via JWT-secured credentials. The system establishes user identity, assigned research groups, and role permissions.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2: Research Project Selection:} The authenticated user selects an active research project (e.g., \texttt{P7-CUO-}\allowbreak\texttt{2026}). Project configuration catalogs (allowable precursors, biomass, deposition methods) are loaded into context.
    \item \textbf{Stage 3: Definition and Recording of Synthesis Parameters:} Researchers configure experimental run parameters (temperatures, concentrations, flow rates, stoichiometric ratios) validated against admissible physical bounds.
    \item \textbf{Stage 4: Creation of Experiments and Associated Samples:} The platform persists the experiment record and generates unique sample identifiers (e.g., \texttt{P7-CUO-S01}) associated with specific substrates and deposition runs.
    \item \textbf{Stage 5: Upload and Storage of Characterization Files:} Raw characterization files (XRD, UV-Vis, FTIR, SEM, $I\text{--}V$) are uploaded. The system calculates running SHA-256 hashes, detects duplicates, and stores raw streams in partitioned storage.
    \item \textbf{Stage 6: Processing of Experimental Data:} The tabular ingestion engine sniffs file headers, removes non-numeric tokens, isolates coordinate data, and applies Savitzky-Golay signal smoothing.
    \item \textbf{Stage 7: Extraction of Relevant Scientific Features:} Peak locations, FWHM values, absorption edges, and current-voltage slopes are extracted from conditioned datasets.
    \item \textbf{Stage 8: Calculation and Storage of Material Properties:} Physical property engines evaluate Scherrer crystallite sizes, Tauc optical bandgaps, and Ohmic electrical conductivities, persisting results to \texttt{calculated\_properties}.
    \item \textbf{Stage 9: Preparation of Datasets for Statistical Analysis and Machine Learning:} Validated experimental features and calculated properties are compiled into structured tabular ML datasets, with explicit partitioning between training and validation sets.
    \item \textbf{Stage 10: Model Training, Evaluation, and Prediction:} Machine learning algorithms (Random Forest, XGBoost) are trained and cross-validated. Performance metrics ($R^2$, RMSE, MAE) are recorded, and trained models are deployed for property prediction.
    \item \textbf{Stage 11: Design of Experiments (DOE) and Candidate Generation (Optional Stage):} \textit{Optional/Exploratory Stage:} The platform employs factorial or Latin Hypercube sampling to recommend candidate experimental parameters aimed at target property optimization.
    \item \textbf{Stage 12: Review of Results and Laboratory Validation:} Researchers inspect dashboards, evaluate ML predictions against physical bench results, and export comprehensive PDF dossiers to close the experimental loop.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Database Architecture and Entity-Relationship Model}

The GreenSynth Analytics database is architected in Third Normal Form (3NF) to guarantee referential integrity, eliminate data redundancy, and maintain strict traceability from raw instrument files back to experimental synthesis runs.

\subsection{Entities, Schemas, and Relational Structure}

The database architecture is structured across eight functional relational domains:

\subsubsection{1. User Accounts, Roles, and Research Group Membership}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{users}: Stores researcher credentials, full name, hashed passwords (Argon2/bcrypt), email address, system role (\texttt{admin}, \texttt{researcher}, \texttt{student}, \texttt{viewer}), active status, and audit timestamps.
    \item \texttt{research\_groups}: Defines laboratory research teams, principal investigator ownership, and institutional affiliations.
    \item \texttt{group\_memberships}: Associative table mapping users to research groups with group-specific roles and invitation tracking.
    \item \texttt{invitations}: Manages pending, accepted, or expired research group invitation tokens.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{2. Research Projects and Configuration Catalogs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{projects}: Top-level organizational entity storing project code (e.g., \texttt{P7-CUO-}\allowbreak\texttt{2026}), title, description, research group foreign key, and lifecycle state (\texttt{active}, \texttt{archived}).
    \item \texttt{project\_definitions}: Defines project-specific parameter bounds, target material families, and operational constraints.
    \item \texttt{materials\_catalog}, \texttt{biomass\_catalog}, \texttt{solvents\_catalog}, \texttt{synthesis\_methods\_catalog}: Standardized reference catalogs defining acceptable botanical extracts, precursor salts, solvents, and deposition techniques.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{3. Experiments, Parameters, and Samples}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{experiments}: Records individual synthesis runs, linking to parent project, creator ID, run status (\texttt{PLANNED}, \texttt{COMPLETED}, \texttt{FAILED}), and run timestamps.
    \item \texttt{parameter\_definitions}: Defines expected experimental variables (e.g., \texttt{substrate\_temperature}, \texttt{precursor\_molarity}), measurement units, and admissible minimum/maximum bounds.
    \item \texttt{experiment\_parameters}: Key-value relational entity storing actual recorded synthesis values for an experiment, enforcing composite uniqueness on \texttt{(experiment\_id, parameter\_definition\_id)}.
    \item \texttt{samples}: Represents physical fabricated specimens resulting from an experiment, storing unique sample codes (e.g., \texttt{P7-CUO-S01}), storage locations, substrate type, and creation date.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{4. Characterization Records and Raw Files}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{characterizations}: Associates a physical sample with an analytical measurement event, recording instrument type (\texttt{XRD}, \texttt{UV\_VIS}, \texttt{FTIR}, \texttt{SEM}, \texttt{ELECTRICAL}), operator, and timestamp.
    \item \texttt{raw\_files}: Stores metadata for ingested instrument files, including original filename, MIME type, file size, SHA-256 cryptographic checksum, and storage file path. Enforces unique constraint on \texttt{checksum\_sha256}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{5. Analysis Runs, Processed Files, and Extracted Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{analysis\_runs}: Tracks execution of scientific derivation algorithms, storing routine name (e.g., \texttt{scherrer\_crystallite\_size}), execution status, input parameters, and runtime duration.
    \item \texttt{processed\_files}: References conditioned, filtered coordinate datasets generated from raw files.
    \item \texttt{xrd\_peaks}: Stores individual fitted diffraction peaks ($2\theta_0$, FWHM $\beta$, intensity $I$, Miller indices $(hkl)$).
    \item \texttt{ftir\_annotations}, \texttt{sem\_metadata}, \texttt{sem\_measurements}: Stores domain-specific annotations including infrared vibrational band assignments and SEM grain boundary diameter measurements.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{6. Calculated Material Properties}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{calculated\_properties}: Centralized repository storing standardized physical metrics derived from analysis runs. Records property name (e.g., \texttt{optical\_bandgap}, \texttt{crystallite\_size}, \texttt{electrical\_conductivity}), numeric value, unit of measure, measurement uncertainty, and algorithm reference.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{7. Machine Learning Datasets, Models, and Predictions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ml\_datasets}: Defines curated feature-target matrices assembled for machine learning.
    \item \texttt{ml\_dataset\_records}: Associative table linking specific samples and calculated properties to an ML dataset.
    \item \texttt{ml\_training\_runs}: Tracks training job parameters, train/test split ratios, and cross-validation configurations.
    \item \texttt{ml\_models}: Stores serialized model artifacts (weights, pipelines), algorithm type (Random Forest, XGBoost), and validation scores ($R^2$, RMSE, MAE).
    \item \texttt{ml\_predictions}: Records model inference outputs for hypothetical synthesis parameter configurations.
    \item \texttt{prediction\_validations}: Maps predicted material properties against subsequent physical laboratory measurements, tracking prediction error and model health.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{8. Experimental Design (DOE) and Recommendations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{does}, \texttt{proposed\_experiments}, \texttt{doe\_analyses}: Manages Design of Experiments exploration matrices and candidate synthesis conditions.
    \item \texttt{recommendations}, \texttt{optimization\_runs}: Stores algorithmic candidate parameter suggestions generated via multi-objective Bayesian or factorial optimization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Constraints, Data Integrity, and Access Security}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Referential Integrity:} Foreign keys employ explicit cascading actions (\texttt{ON DELETE CASCADE} for dependent records such as parameters and analysis runs; \texttt{ON DELETE RESTRICT} for core user and project definitions).
    \item \textbf{Cryptographic Deduplication:} The unique constraint on \texttt{raw\_files.checksum\_sha256} prevents identical characterization files from being stored multiple times.
    \item \textbf{Parameter Bounds Constraints:} Check constraints validate numeric ranges (e.g., substrate temperature $T_{\text{sub}} > 0$, solution $\text{pH} \in [0, 14]$).
    \item \textbf{Synthetic vs.\ Experimental Data Segregation:} The schema explicitly incorporates an \texttt{is\_synthetic} boolean flag across experimental and dataset records. This guarantees that synthetic benchmark suites (used for stress-testing ML pipelines) are never conflated with physical laboratory measurements.
    \item \textbf{Audit Logging:} The \texttt{audit\_logs} table captures an immutable record of all data modifications, logging user ID, timestamp, IP address, target table, and JSON diffs of modified fields.
\end{itemize}


\section{Modules Description}

\subsection{User Authentication and Research Access Module}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Purpose:} Governs secure platform entry, identity verification, role-based authorization, and project-level data segregation.
    \item \textbf{Inputs:} User credentials (email, password), invitation tokens, role assignment requests.
    \item \textbf{Processing Logic:} Passwords are verified against stored hashes using Argon2/bcrypt. Upon successful authentication, the module issues signed HMAC-SHA256 JWT access tokens containing user claims, role privileges, and expiration times. Endpoint dependency guards inspect tokens on every incoming request, verifying group membership and project ownership before granting read/write permissions.
    \item \textbf{Outputs:} Signed JWT bearer tokens, user profile contexts, authorization rejection exceptions (HTTP 401/403).
    \item \textbf{Interactions:} Interacts directly with all downstream modules by injecting verified user and project context into every API request.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Data and Characterization Management Module}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Purpose:} Provides comprehensive lifecycle management for synthesis experiments, physical samples, and raw characterization files.
    \item \textbf{Inputs:} Synthesis condition parameters, sample metadata, raw ASCII/binary characterization files (XRD, UV-Vis, FTIR, SEM, $I\text{--}V$).
    \item \textbf{Processing Logic:} Validates input parameters against catalog bounds; generates unique sample identifiers; streams uploaded files through a SHA-256 cryptographic hashing pipeline; rejects duplicate files; sniffs column delimiters; cleans numeric coordinates; and writes files to partitioned storage.
    \item \textbf{Outputs:} Persisted experiment records, linked sample entities, sanitized coordinate datasets, raw file audit metadata.
    \item \textbf{Interactions:} Feeds conditioned coordinate arrays into the Scientific Analytics and Machine-Learning Module.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scientific Analytics and Machine-Learning Module}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Purpose:} Automates mathematical property derivations, executes cross-validated regression modeling, and generates candidate experimental conditions.
    \item \textbf{Inputs:} Conditioned spectral/diffraction datasets, sample parameter vectors, ML model hyperparameter configurations.
    \item \textbf{Processing Logic:} Executes digital signal processing (Savitzky-Golay smoothing, baseline estimation); performs non-linear curve fitting for Scherrer crystallite sizing; calculates Tauc optical bandgaps via inflection point analysis; derives electrical conductivities from $I\text{--}V$ slopes; curates ML training matrices; trains Random Forest and XGBoost regressors under 5-fold cross-validation; and evaluates regression metrics ($R^2$, RMSE, MAE).
    \item \textbf{Outputs:} Standardized calculated properties, regression model weights, cross-validation metrics, interactive Recharts visualizations, and downloadable PDF dossiers.
    \item \textbf{Interactions:} Consumes data from the Characterization Management Module and writes derived properties back to the central database.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{RESULTS AND DISCUSSION}

\section{Output of the System}

This section presents the functional outputs generated by the GreenSynth Analytics platform and evaluates its performance in managing materials research data, executing automated characterization processing, and supporting data-driven scientific analysis.

\subsection{User Interface and Research Management}

The platform realizes a responsive, role-differentiated web interface designed for research laboratory operations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authentication and Role-Based Access:} The application gatekeeps access through a secure authentication portal. Upon successful verification of researcher credentials, the backend issues a signed JSON Web Token (JWT) encoding role permissions. As demonstrated in system operations, \texttt{Administrator} accounts access system-wide configuration, user management, and catalog bounds; \texttt{Researcher} accounts manage assigned research groups, create projects, and train predictive models; while \texttt{Student} accounts are restricted to assigned project experiments, sample logging, and raw file uploads.
    \item \textbf{Executive Dashboard:} The primary landing interface (Figure \ref{fig:ss_dashboard}) consolidates real-time project metrics, including active research initiatives (e.g., \texttt{P7-CUO-2026}), completed synthesis experiment counts, active sample inventories, pending characterization pipelines, and recent audit activity.
    \item \textbf{Project and Experiment Management:} The project management view (Figure \ref{fig:ss_experiments}) provides hierarchical navigation from top-level research initiatives down to individual deposition runs. Researchers configure experimental run parameters, monitor sample lineages, and review procedural execution logs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Data Management and Scientific Traceability}

The core operational achievement of the data management module is establishing end-to-end relational traceability across the materials lifecycle:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Precursor and Parameter Association:} Each physical synthesis experiment is explicitly bound to validated operational parameters (precursor salt molarity, leaf extract concentration, spray flow rate, carrier pressure, and substrate temperature) stored within \texttt{experiment\_parameters}.
    \item \textbf{Sample Identity and Physical Lineage:} Fabricated specimens are instantiated as distinct entities within \texttt{samples}, linking physical substrates and storage locations directly to their originating deposition run.
    \item \textbf{Characterization Vault and Audit Trail:} Raw instrument files uploaded by researchers are cryptographically indexed using SHA-256 digests in \texttt{raw\_files}. This ensures that characterization files cannot be accidentally modified, overwritten, or disassociated from their corresponding physical sample.
    \item \textbf{Calculated Property Traceability:} Every derived physical metric stored in \texttt{calculated\_properties} maintains an immutable foreign key reference back to the originating \texttt{analysis\_runs} record, capturing the exact algorithm version, input parameters, and execution timestamp used to produce the result.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Characterization Data and Scientific Visualization}

GreenSynth Analytics integrates dedicated analytical pipelines for the multi-technique characterization suite utilized in copper oxide research. For each technique, the platform enforces a clear separation between raw measurements, processed signals, extracted features, and standardized calculated properties:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Raw Measurement:} Two-column ASCII coordinate stream of diffraction angle ($2\theta$) and photon counts ($I$).
        \item \textit{Processed Signal:} Background-corrected, Savitzky-Golay smoothed diffractogram isolating Bragg diffraction peaks.
        \item \textit{Extracted Features:} Peak centroid locations ($2\theta_0$), Full Width at Half Maximum ($\beta$), and integrated peak intensities for monoclinic tenorite $\text{CuO}$ reflections—specifically the $(\bar{1}11)$ and $(111)$ doublet at $2\theta \approx 35.5^\circ$ and $38.7^\circ$ (Figure \ref{fig:ss_xrd}).
        \item \textit{Calculated Properties:} Average crystallite size ($D = 19.10\text{ nm}$) evaluated via the Scherrer equation and dislocation density ($\delta = 2.74 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}^{-2}$).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{UV-Visible (UV-Vis) Optical Spectroscopy:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Raw Measurement:} Spectral coordinates of incident wavelength ($\lambda \in [200, 1100]\text{ nm}$) and optical absorbance ($A$).
        \item \textit{Processed Signal:} Baseline-subtracted optical absorption spectrum converted to photon energy ($h\nu$) and Tauc absorption transform ($(\alpha h\nu)^2$).
        \item \textit{Extracted Features:} First-derivative maximum $\frac{d}{d(h\nu)}[(\alpha h\nu)^2]$ pinpointing the fundamental absorption edge inflection coordinate ($h\nu_{\text{infl}} \approx 1.82\text{ eV}$).
        \item \textit{Calculated Properties:} Direct optical bandgap ($E_g = 1.74\text{ eV}$) derived from the linear tangent regression intercept with the energy axis (Figure \ref{fig:ss_tauc}).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Raw Measurement:} Wavenumber ($\bar{\nu} \in [400, 4000]\text{ cm}^{-1}$) vs.\ transmittance percentage ($\%T$).
        \item \textit{Processed Signal:} Baseline-normalized vibrational spectrum with automated valley detection.
        \item \textit{Extracted Features:} Prominent infrared vibrational absorption bands at $530\text{ cm}^{-1}$ and $588\text{ cm}^{-1}$ corresponding to the characteristic high-frequency $A_u$ and $B_u$ vibrational stretching modes of the monoclinic $\text{Cu--O}$ lattice bond.
        \item \textit{Calculated Properties:} Confirmation of tenorite phase purity, as indicated by the complete absence of residual organic ester ($\text{C=O}$, $\sim 1730\text{ cm}^{-1}$) or aliphatic hydrocarbon ($\text{C--H}$, $\sim 2920\text{ cm}^{-1}$) bands, proving complete pyrolytic ligand elimination during spray pyrolysis.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) Morphology:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Raw Measurement:} High-resolution uncompressed 8-bit greyscale electron micrograph.
        \item \textit{Processed Signal:} Contrast-enhanced, adaptive thresholded image isolating grain boundary topologies (Figure \ref{fig:ss_sem}).
        \item \textit{Extracted Features:} Binary morphological grain contours and equivalent circular diameter distributions.
        \item \textit{Calculated Properties:} Mean grain diameter ($d_{\text{grain}} \approx 52.4\text{ nm} \pm 8.2\text{ nm}$), confirming uniform nanoscale grain coalescence without macroscopic pinholes or micro-cracks.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Electrical Current-Voltage ($I\text{--}V$) Transport:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Raw Measurement:} Voltage sweep array ($V \in [-10\text{ V}, +10\text{ V}]$) and measured current ($I$).
        \item \textit{Processed Signal:} Linearized $I\text{--}V$ characteristic curve with baseline offset adjustment.
        \item \textit{Extracted Features:} Slope of the linear regression line ($m = 1/R$) exhibiting Ohmic linearity ($R^2 = 0.9994$).
        \item \textit{Calculated Properties:} Room-temperature electrical resistivity ($\rho = 1.550\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$) and electrical conductivity ($\sigma = 0.645\text{ S/cm}$) evaluated using two-probe planar thin-film geometry.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Analysis and Discussion of Model Performance}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comparison with Mean Baseline:} The dummy mean baseline model predicts the global training mean for all queries, yielding an expected $R^2 \approx 0$ ($R^2 = -0.0050$) and high error ($\text{RMSE} = 0.2625\text{ S/cm}$). All trained models substantially outperform the baseline, confirming that the pipeline captures non-trivial correlations between synthesis parameters and target properties.
    \item \textbf{Non-Linear vs.\ Linear Regressors:} Standard Linear and Ridge Regression achieve modest predictive fidelity ($R^2 \approx 0.302$). This limitation arises because thin-film pyrolytic growth kinetics exhibit complex, non-linear dependencies (e.g., thermal activation thresholds and extract-induced complexation saturation). In contrast, ensemble tree-based architectures—Gradient Boosting ($R^2 = 0.6360$) and Random Forest ($R^2 = 0.6685$)—effectively capture multi-variable interactions and non-monotonic trends.
    \item \textbf{Top-Performing Model:} The Random Forest Regressor achieved the highest cross-validation coefficient of determination ($R^2 = 0.6685$) and lowest predictive errors ($\text{RMSE} = 0.1501\text{ S/cm}$, $\text{MAE} = 0.1214\text{ S/cm}$). As shown in the model leaderboard (Figure \ref{fig:ss_ml}), bagging mitigates overfitting on smaller materials informatics sample sizes.
    \item \textbf{Methodological Limitations of Synthetic Data:} It is essential to emphasize that these benchmark metrics demonstrate the functional correctness and numerical behavior of the implemented ML pipeline on simulated benchmark data. They do not claim physical predictive fidelity for unmeasured laboratory specimens. True material forecasting accuracy requires future model retraining on large, independently collected physical datasets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Industrial Benefits and Impact}

GreenSynth Analytics presents tangible utility for academic materials science laboratories, research institutes, and industrial research and development groups specializing in semiconductor thin films and functional nanomaterials.

\subsection{Improved Research Data Management}
By organizing experimental variables, physical sample identifiers, raw characterization scans, and derived metrics into an integrated relational database, the platform prevents data fragmentation. Researchers can immediately query historical synthesis runs, audit parameter configurations, and retrieve original diffractograms without relying on informal paper records.

\subsection{Support for Materials Research}
The platform accelerates materials discovery by directly connecting physical synthesis conditions to electronic, optical, and structural properties. In the context of copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$) semiconductor thin films, researchers can systematically examine how botanical extract concentration, solution pH, and spray deposition temperatures influence tenorite crystallite size, optical bandgap tuning ($1.7\text{--}2.1\text{ eV}$), and electrical conductivity.

\subsection{Data-Driven Experimental Planning}
Rather than relying solely on trial-and-error experimentation, researchers can leverage the integrated Design of Experiments (DOE) generator and machine learning regressors to identify promising synthesis regimes. Generating candidate parameters through systematic Central Composite Design enables researchers to prioritize high-yield experimental runs, minimizing chemical reagent consumption and laboratory equipment run-time.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Collaboration}
Scientific reproducibility requires absolute transparency in data processing. GreenSynth Analytics records the exact mathematical algorithms, smoothing windows, and regression intercepts used for every calculated property. Furthermore, role-based access control and automated ReportLab PDF dossier generation facilitate seamless, peer-verifiable collaboration between principal investigators, research scientists, and students.

\subsection{Potential Industrial Applications}
In industrial manufacturing and pilot-scale thin-film coating operations, platforms of this nature can support continuous quality assurance, batch-to-batch consistency tracking, and chemical formulation screening. The automated parser and property extraction engines can be integrated into production lines to monitor film conductivity and crystalline phase purity in real time.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Validation}
To maintain academic integrity, several boundaries of the current implementation must be highlighted:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Benchmark Data:} The machine learning benchmark results reported in Section 7.1.4 were derived using synthetic P7 dataset suites developed for software verification and pipeline stress-testing. While these results prove the technical robustness of the algorithms, they do not establish empirical predictive accuracy for physical materials.
    \item \textbf{Requirement for Empirical Validation:} Future research phases must involve collecting large, physical, experimental datasets across varying deposition conditions to retrain and validate ML regressors against physical measurements.
    \item \textbf{Production Readiness:} Transitioning from a research laboratory platform to full industrial deployment will necessitate enterprise data governance, horizontal database scaling, automated instrument IoT integration, and long-term security auditing.
\end{enumerate}


\chapter{CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE}

\section{Summary of work}

This interdisciplinary project realized a dual-track investigation bridging physical materials science and scientific informatics:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Part A: Physical Materials Science Track (CuO Thin Films by Spray Pyrolysis):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Botanical Extraction:} Executed a solid-liquid Soxhlet extraction protocol isolating natural bioactive polyphenolic ligands from \textit{Morus alba} (mulberry) leaves in ethanol at $65^\circ\text{C}\text{--}70^\circ\text{C}$.
        \item \textbf{Precursor Stoichiometry and Wet Chemistry:} Optimized the chemical precipitation and complexation of divalent copper ions using copper sulfate pentahydrate ($\text{CuSO}_4\cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) in an exact $1:2$ molar stoichiometry. Monitored the dynamic pH trajectory ($2.45 \rightarrow 2.60 \rightarrow 12.36$) to control nucleation kinetics and prevent premature particle agglomeration.
        \item \textbf{Spray Pyrolysis Thin-Film Deposition:} Established the pyrolytic deposition window ($T_{\text{sub}} = 350^\circ\text{C}\text{--}400^\circ\text{C}$, spray rate $3.5\text{ mL/min}$, carrier pressure $1.8\text{ bar}$, nozzle distance $28\text{ cm}$, $25\text{ min}$ duration with a $10\text{ s ON} / 15\text{ s OFF}$ duty cycle) to grow adherent, uniform, monoclinic tenorite $\text{CuO}$ thin films on glass substrates.
        \item \textbf{Physical Characterization:} Evaluated thin films across X-ray diffraction (XRD), UV-Visible spectroscopy, FTIR spectroscopy, FE-SEM surface morphology, and Keithley 2450 two-probe current-voltage ($I\text{--}V$) electrical transport.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Part B: Materials Informatics Software Track (GreenSynth Analytics):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Architecture Realization:} Engineered a full-stack, modular web platform comprising a FastAPI asynchronous Python backend, PostgreSQL relational database (with SQLite for development), SQLAlchemy 2.0 ORM, Pydantic v2 data contracts, and a React 18 / TypeScript single-page application styled with Tailwind CSS.
        \item \textbf{Relational Schema and Data Integrity:} Formulated a 3NF database schema spanning 8 functional domains, guaranteeing bi-directional traceability from raw instrument files to experimental parameters, samples, and calculated properties, backed by SHA-256 cryptographic deduplication.
        \item \textbf{Automated Scientific Engine:} Programmed five core analytical routines that automate signal filtering (Savitzky-Golay), XRD Scherrer crystallite sizing ($D = 19.10\text{ nm}$), UV-Vis Tauc direct optical bandgap extraction ($E_g = 1.74\text{ eV}$), and $I\text{--}V$ Ohmic conductivity derivation ($\sigma = 0.645\text{ S/cm}$).
        \item \textbf{Machine Learning and DOE Integration:} Developed an ML benchmarking pipeline evaluating cross-validated regressors (Random Forest vs.\ XGBoost) and implemented a 29-run Central Composite Design (CCD) generator for systematic synthesis parameter exploration.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Achievements}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standardized Spray Pyrolysis Deposition Envelope:} Established the pyrolytic deposition window ($T_{\text{sub}} = 350^\circ\text{C}\text{--}400^\circ\text{C}$, $3.5\text{ mL/min}$, $1.8\text{ bar}$) preventing substrate thermal quenching and producing adherent, nanocrystalline monoclinic $\text{CuO}$ thin films confirmed via XRD, UV-Vis, FTIR, SEM, and electrical $I\text{--}V$ characterization.
    \item \textbf{Elimination of Research Data Fragmentation:} Replaced disparate paper lab notebooks, manual spreadsheets, and instrument local disks with a centralized, tamper-evident digital repository enforcing physical boundary validation and SHA-256 cryptographic deduplication.
    \item \textbf{Millisecond-Level Automated Scientific Calculations:} Automated property extraction algorithms execute in under $200\text{ ms}$ (compared to 15–30 minutes of manual plotting), achieving high mathematical fidelity ($< 0.6\%$ relative error) when benchmarked directly against industry-standard \textbf{OriginLab 2024}.
    \item \textbf{Integrated Materials Informatics and Machine Learning Pipeline:} Successfully bridged physical synthesis tracking with data science pipelines, demonstrating end-to-end 5-fold cross-validation of predictive regressors ($R^2 = 0.6685$ for Random Forest), systematic 29-run Central Composite Design (CCD) generation, and automated ReportLab PDF dossier generation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Industrial usefulness}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Optoelectronic and Clean Energy Device Manufacturing:} Semiconducting $\text{CuO}$ thin films exhibiting a direct optical bandgap of $\sim 1.74\text{ eV}$ and electrical conductivity of $\sim 0.645\text{ S/cm}$ are prime candidates for p-type absorber layers in thin-film solar cells, photodetectors, electrochromic smart windows, and photocathodes for solar water splitting.
    \item \textbf{Research Data Governance and Quality Control:} In commercial R\&D and pilot manufacturing lines, the platform's immutable SHA-256 checksums, automated audit trails (\texttt{audit\_logs}), and role-based access control fulfill industrial data governance requirements (such as ALCOA+ principles), preventing data manipulation and ensuring audit-readiness.
    \item \textbf{Accelerated High-Throughput Materials Screening:} Automated ingestion, parsing, and dossier generation compress characterization analysis cycles from hours to seconds, significantly lowering labor costs, accelerating high-throughput materials screening, and reducing pilot-line downtime.
    \item \textbf{Batch-to-Batch Process Standardization:} Enables continuous monitoring of chemical formulation parameters and thin-film deposition conditions to maintain quality control across successive fabrication batches.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future enhancements}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Direct Instrument IoT Integration:} Implement automated hardware streaming interfaces using Standard Commands for Programmable Instruments (SCPI), VISA, or OPC-UA protocols, allowing instruments (Keithley SourceMeters, spectrophotometers, diffractometers) to push coordinate streams directly into GreenSynth Analytics without manual file handling.
    \item \textbf{Active Learning and Autonomous Bayesian Optimization:} Upgrade the experimental design module by integrating Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) with Expected Improvement (EI) acquisition functions to autonomously recommend the single next best synthesis experiment, interfacing with automated robotic liquid-handling synthesis stations.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Model Retraining on Large-Scale Multi-Dopant Datasets:} Expand the machine learning dataset beyond synthetic benchmark data by curating physical multi-laboratory datasets covering transition metal dopants (e.g., Al, Zn, Fe, Ni-doped $\text{CuO}$) and alternative deposition methods (spin coating, chemical bath deposition, sol-gel dip coating).
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning for Automated Micrograph Segmentation:} Incorporate computer vision models (such as U-Net or Mask R-CNN) into the SEM module to automate semantic grain boundary segmentation, porosity percentage quantification, and crystallographic defect classification directly from raw electron micrographs.
    \item \textbf{Cloud-Native Enterprise Scaling:} Introduce horizontal microservice orchestration via Kubernetes, object storage integration (Amazon S3 / MinIO), single sign-on (OAuth2 / SAML), and multi-tenant laboratory workspace isolation for institutional and industrial deployments.
\end{enumerate}
